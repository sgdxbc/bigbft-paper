\paragraph{Problem Settings.}
Our work functions as a replicated service for arbitrary state machines, exhibiting tolerance to Byzantine faults akin to conventional BFT protocols while sustaining a favorable storage overhead comparable to distributed databases \lijl{storage overhead is a weird metric to compare}.
Specifically, \sys ensures safety and liveness properties consistent with conventional BFT protocols~\cite{pbft} and a bounded redundancy property analogous to distributed storage systems~\cite{corbett2013spanner}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Safety: The replicated service adheres to linearizability~\cite{herlihy1987axioms}, functioning equivalently to a centralized implementation that processes transactions atomically and sequentially.
    \item Liveness: Clients eventually receive responses to their submitted transactions.
    \item Storage scalability: The total storage resources required for system operation are $O(\text{state size})$.
    The number of nodes within the system does not influence storage consumption.
    This contrasts with typical BFT protocols where the storage complexity is $O(\text{state size} \times \text{number of nodes})$.

\end{itemize}
\sys operates with $3f+1$ nodes and guarantees the aforementioned properties when up to $f$ nodes exhibit Byzantine faults and behave arbitrarily.
Similar to numerous prior studies, \sys achieves these properties under a partially synchronous network model.
Outside the period of synchrony, the protocol may experience a lack of progress, but safety and storage scalability remain uncompromised.

\paragraph{Blockchain Storage Consumption.}
The rapidly expanding state sizes of prevalent permissionless blockchains are a well-established phenomenon.
From 2019 to the present, the blockchain states of Bitcoin and Ethereum have grown from approximately 180 GB to 660 GB\footnote{\url{https://www.blockchain.com/explorer/charts/blocks-size}} and 1410 GB\footnote{\url{https://etherscan.io/chartsync/chaindefault}}, respectively (\autoref{fig:states-grow}).
Considering the substantial number of nodes in these blockchains (22,275 for Bitcoin\footnote{\url{https://bitnodes.io/}}, 11,251 for Ethereum\footnote{\url{https://etherscan.io/nodetracker}}), the aggregate storage consumed by their nodes is around 15 PB.
Given the exorbitant cost of on-chain storage~\cite{balduf2022dude} (3.28 ETH per MB~\cite{palau2018storing}, exceeding 8000 USD at current exchange rates), the aforementioned blockchain states likely exhibit the \emph{slowest} rate of increase.
The growth in state size significantly outpaces the reduction in storage media costs.
SSD prices in 2019 were only $1.3\text{--}2\times$ higher than in 2025\footnote{\url{https://www.qootec.com/news/2025\%20ssd\%20price\%20trends.html}}, whereas state sizes in 2025 are $3.6\text{--}7.8\times$ larger than in 2019, exacerbating the decreasing affordability of operating blockchain nodes.
\lijl{For permissionless blockchains, we can use barrier of entry as an argument.}

Although permissioned or consortium blockchains in data centers have greater storage capacity, they require even larger blockchain states.
Beyond well-known financial uses~\cite{qi2021bidl,lokhava2019fast}, prior work has also proposed deploying various big data applications on blockchains, like mobile crowdsensing in industrial IoT~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, edge computing~\cite{xu2018making}, crime evidence~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design}, medical and educational records~\cite{sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss}, machine learning~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain}, and intelligent transportation and smart cities~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain}.
Blockchain's strong security makes it ideal and widely researched for securing different phases of processing large volumes of data, such as data collection~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, sharing~\cite{xu2018making}, management~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design,sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss}, and analytics~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain}, as well as protecting privacy during processing~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain} in environments with unreliable or mutually distrusting participants.

These extensive data blockchains can accumulate very large states.
NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH, a dataset recorded at a trading matching engine, reportedly has a total size of 20 TB and grows at 4.13 GB per day~\cite{laughlin2014information}.

High-frequency stock market data can accumulate as much as 20-40 GB daily~\cite{rabindrajit2024high}.
Furthermore, medical records typically range from MB to tens of MB in size~\cite{garcia2011mantisgrid} due to their multimodal content, while public medical record collections can contain 40,000-200,000 entries~\cite{pollard2018eicu,johnson2023mimic} and occupy terabytes of storage.
These represent the dimensions of single-party datasets. In consortium blockchains, multiple participants (e.g., exchanges or hospitals) contribute their data, each potentially comparable in size to these datasets.
Consequently, this can lead to blockchain states of terabyte or even petabyte scale, exceeding the storage capabilities of individual blockchain nodes.

The essential point is that achieving storage scalability is critical for blockchains in diverse settings, alleviating the requirement for individual nodes to host the entire state.
In storage-scalable blockchains like \sys, the maximum operational state size depends on the total storage supply collaboratively provided by all nodes, rather than the storage capacity of each individual node.
When more nodes join the system, the storage overhead can \emph{scale out} to their storage resources, reducing storage pressure on existing nodes and enabling the system to manage larger states, similar to conventional distributed databases~\cite{corbett2013spanner}.
This characteristic is crucial for enabling blockchains to handle massive big data states, akin to distributed databases.

\paragraph{Blockchain Systems with Scalable Storage (\autoref{tab:compare}).}
Several prior works enable operation with nodes storing partial states through various mechanisms.
In blockchain databases~\cite{ruan2021blockchains} such as \textit{BlockchainDB}~\cite{el2019blockchaindb} and \textsc{GriDB}~\cite{hong2024gridb}, the state is composed of multiple tables.
Each table (or more granular table partition) is stored by a subset of nodes, and authenticated data structures~\cite{martel2004general, tamassia2003authenticated} are utilized to securely process queries across tables (e.g., join operations)~\cite{zhang2015integridb,hong2024gridb}.
More general-purpose sharded blockchains adopt this model, organizing nodes into multiple \emph{committees} responsible for maintaining distinct state shards.
These approaches typically employ multiple instances of fully-replicated BFT protocols (common choices include PBFT~\cite{pbft} and ByzCoin~\cite{kogias2016enhancing}), one within each committee, and address the atomicity of transactions spanning committees using specialized strategies.

Next, we elaborate on these sharded blockchain systems.
\textsc{Elastico}~\cite{luu2016secure} establishes the groundwork for scalable sharded blockchains.
It employs epoch-based randomization of committees.
At the start of each epoch, every node computes a proof-of-work (PoW) to acquire membership in a random committee.
Subsequently, the members of each committee execute a permissioned BFT protocol locally to achieve agreement on the shard they manage and then disseminate the consensus result to other committees to attain global agreement.
Since each committee randomly selects the minimum number of nodes required to ensure an honest supermajority with high probability, the committee size remains constant irrespective of the total node count.
Consequently, \textsc{Elastico} achieves storage scalability: its redundancy is equivalent to the committee size and is unaffected by the number of nodes.

Building upon the advancements of \textsc{Elastico}, OmniLedger~\cite{kokoris2018omniledger} enhances several security features by substituting the PoW-based committee assignment with RandHound~\cite{syta2017scalable}, a decentralized randomness solution utilizing verifiable random functions (VRFs).
This allows OmniLedger to extend the epoch duration considerably (e.g., to one day) and reduce the storage overhead for nodes more practically than \textsc{Elastico}.
Subsequent work~\cite{dang2019towards} further integrates trusted execution environments (TEEs) to bolster intra-committee consensus with improved resilience.
As an alternative to TEEs, RapidChain~\cite{zamani2018rapidchain} employs a synchronous BFT protocol for greater resilience and explicitly states its ability to tolerate $\frac{1}{3}$ Byzantine faults, the same as conventional BFT protocols.
Both of these works achieve $\frac{1}{2}$ fault tolerance within committees, enabling a significant reduction in committee size while maintaining similar probabilistic security guarantees (\eg, 60 vs. 800 nodes for $\frac{1}{4}$ faulty nodes and a $2^{-20}$ probability of a faulty committee~\cite{dang2019towards}).
GearBox~\cite{david2022gearbox} investigates flexible liveness resilience to minimize committee size, starting with small committees that consistently ensure safety but only guarantee liveness when most or all members are honest.
It then continuously monitors committee liveness and expands committees lacking progress.
These systems share a similar fundamental structure to \textsc{Elastico} but achieve lower redundancy and improved storage scalability by refining the intra-committee consensus or committee formation mechanisms.

Furthermore, certain sharded blockchains do not require explicit committee formation to achieve their security properties.
This category includes the previously mentioned blockchain database systems, as well as SharPer~\cite{amiri2021sharper} and \textsc{ByShard}~\cite{hellings2021byshard}, which leverage pre-determined fault-tolerant clusters to provide deterministic safety guarantees, in contrast to the probabilistic guarantees offered by the previously discussed systems.
\textsc{GeoBFT}~\cite{gupta2020resilientdb} further optimizes for geographically distributed clusters, enhancing the performance of global blockchain deployments.
Monoxide~\cite{wang2019monoxide} introduces an asynchronous consensus zone concept, which can be viewed as arbitrarily sized committees.
It proposes a novel PoW mining scheme that enables mining power to concurrently operate across multiple zones and tolerate up to $\frac{1}{2}$ faulty mining power globally.

\paragraph{The Trade-off Between Security and Scalability.}
As illustrated in \autoref{tab:compare}, all prior systems fail to simultaneously achieve safety, liveness, and storage scalability.
Conventional BFT protocols and certain blockchain databases~\cite{peng2020falcondb} fully replicate their states across all nodes, ensuring safety and liveness but lacking storage scalability.
\textit{BlockchainDB}, SharPer, and \textsc{GeoBFT} necessitate pre-defined fault-tolerant clusters.
They can only tolerate a fraction of faulty nodes within each cluster and fail when the total number of faulty nodes overwhelms any single cluster.
\textsc{Elastico}, OmniLedger, and GearBox's fixed-size, randomly-sampled committees are designed to handle low global fault rates (\eg, $\frac{1}{4}$).
Increasing the fault fraction without adjusting the committee size leads to immediate safety and liveness failures.
To maintain these properties, the committee size must be adaptively increased based on the actual fault fraction to ensure that committees contain no more than $\frac{1}{3}$ faulty members with high probability.
For instance, with a $\frac{1}{4}$ fault probability, sampling 378 nodes from 1000 is required to achieve a $10^{-6}$ faulty committee probability, whereas a $\frac{3}{10}$ fault probability necessitates sampling at least 798 nodes for the same probabilistic safety and liveness guarantees\footnote{These numbers are derived with hypergeometric distribution~\cite{hypergeometric-distr} as formulated in the original works.}.
As the fault probability approaches the desired $\frac{1}{3}$, the required number of sampled nodes nears the total number of nodes, and the system reverts to a fully replicated architecture, losing storage scalability.
The other systems employing randomized committees rely on additional assumptions for their safety guarantees, such as trusted hardware or synchronous network conditions.
Monoxide appears to secure all three properties concurrently.
However, if we adhere to the work's assumption that professional mining facilities mining across all zones dominate the mining power, storage scalability is lost because nodes must store states from every zone.
Conversely, if nodes engage in solo mining~\cite{bitcoin_solo_mining} and operate on single zones, individual zones become susceptible to subversion by faulty mining power, resulting in the loss of safety and liveness.
Ultimately, no prior system can simultaneously achieve all three properties.

\paragraph{Limitations of Processing Sharding.}
The shortcomings of these prior systems stem from a fundamental challenge: the fault tolerance of the system is determined by the committee size, whereas conventional BFT settings mandate tolerating $\frac{1}{3}$ faulty nodes, the quantity of which is a function of the total node count.
If the committee size is independent of the total number of nodes, then security properties are compromised, and the system can no longer tolerate a number of faults proportional to the system's scale (as indicated by \emph{No} in the \emph{Safety?} and/or \emph{Liveness?} columns of \autoref{tab:compare}).
Conversely, if the committee size increases with system growth, then storage consumption also rises, and storage scalability is lost (as indicated by \emph{No} in the \emph{Scalable storage?} column).
In essence, processing sharding necessitates a choice between these two options, resulting in undesirable trade-offs among the desired properties.

Scaling out blockchain state has been the recurring topic of blockchain databases.
As the blockchain databases keep operating, the size of content they store may ever grow.

\paragraph{Sharded blockchains}
One layer down, the blockchains can divide the stored data into shards\cite{corbett2013spanner} and each node stores only one or a subset of the shards.
However, without the whole state, the nodes can no longer process every transaction.
EIP-4844~\cite{eip4844} of Ethereum bypasses this limitation by sharding \emph{blob}, a special kind of block data only whose (succinct) commitment is fully replicated and accessible by the transactions.
This approach is not desirable by blockchain databases which requires the whole data to present in the state.
