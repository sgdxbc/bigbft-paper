\paragraph{Problem settings.}
Our work can be used as a replicated service for arbitrary state machines, tolerating Byzantine faults as common BFT protocols while maintaining a desirable storage overhead as distributed databases.
In detail, \sys provides the safety and liveness properties following common BFT protocols~\cite{pbft} and the bounded redundancy property following the distributed storage systems~\cite{corbett2013spanner}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Safety: the replicated service satisfies linearizability~\cite{herlihy1987axioms}, behaving equivalently to a centralized implementation that processes transactions atomically once at a time.
    \item Liveness: the clients are eventually replied for their requested transactions.
    \item Storage scalability: the overall storage resources required to operate the system is $O(state\ size)$.
    The number of nodes in the system do not affect the storage consumption.
    This is in the contrary of normal BFT protocols where the storage complexity is $O(state\ size\times{}number\ of\ node)$.
    \lijl{Either this definition, or that the overall state capacity scales with total storage size, while traditional replication is bounded by the node with the smallest storage.}
\end{itemize}
\sys operates with $3f+1$ nodes and can ensure the above properties when up to $f$ nodes are Byzantine faulty and behave arbitrarily.
Similar to many previous works, \sys these properties in partial synchrony network model.
Beyond the period of synchrony, the protocol can be lack of progress, but safety and storage scalability are not violated.

\paragraph{How much storage space a blockchain may consume?}
It's already not news that popular permissionless blockchains are managing rapidly growing states.
From 2019 to present, Bitcoin and Ethereum blockchain states are growing from both about 180 GB to 660 GB\footnote{\url{https://www.blockchain.com/explorer/charts/blocks-size}} and 1410 GB\footnote{\url{https://etherscan.io/chartsync/chaindefault}} respectively (\autoref{fig:states-grow}).
Given the large number of nodes of these blockchains (22275 for Bitcoin\footnote{\url{https://bitnodes.io/}}, 11251 for Ethereum\footnote{\url{https://etherscan.io/nodetracker}}), both of whose nodes have been consuming around 15 PB storage totally.
Considering on-chain storage has been insanely expensive~\cite{balduf2022dude} (3.28 ETH per MB~\cite{palau2018storing} which is over 8000USD today), these blockchain states are probably the ones with the \emph{lowest} increasing speed.
The increasing of state sizes is much faster than the decreasing of storage media cost.
SSD price in 2019 is only 1.3-2$\times$ to it in 2025\footnote{\url{https://www.qootec.com/news/2025\%20ssd\%20price\%20trends.html}}, while the state sizes in 2025 is 3.6-7.8$\times$ to back in 2019, rendering the degrading affordability of operating blockchain nodes.
\lijl{For permissionless blockchains, we can use barrier of entry as an argument.}

While permissioned or consortium blockchains in data center have more abundant storage resources, they demand even larger blockchain states.
Other than the well-known financial usages~\cite{qi2021bidl,lokhava2019fast}, previous works have also proposed to deploy various big data applications on blockchains like mobile crowdsensing in industrial IoT~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, edge computing~\cite{xu2018making}, crime evidences~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design}, medical and educational records~\cite{sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss}, machine learning~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain}, and intelligent transportation and smart city~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain}.
Blockchain is favored for its strict security guarantee, and has been widely researched for securing data collection~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, sharing~\cite{xu2018making}, management~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design,sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss} and analytics~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain} and preserving privacy during processing~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain} in the environments where participates are unreliable or mutually untrusted.

These big data blockchains can incur very large states.
NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH, a dataset recorded at a trading matching engine, is reported to have 20 TB of total size and 4.13 GB per day~\cite{laughlin2014information}.
\lijl{4 GB growth per day?} \gd{Correct.}
The dataset for high-frequency stock market can go as high as 20-40 GB per day~\cite{rabindrajit2024high}.
Also, the medical records are usually in MB or even tens of MB scale~\cite{garcia2011mantisgrid} due to their multimodal contents, while public medical record datasets may contain up to 40,000-200,000 records~\cite{pollard2018eicu,johnson2023mimic} and occupy TB level storage.
These are just the sizes of one-party datasets. In consortium blockchains, multiple participates (\eg exchanges or hospitals) contribute their data, each of which could be of a similar size of these datasets.
This results in TB or even PB scale blockchain states that are beyond the storage capacity of single blockchain nodes.

The takeaway is that, for blockchains under various settings, it's crucial for them to achieve storage scalability, releasing the nodes from the burden of hosting the full states by themselves.
In storage-scalable blockchains like \sys, the maximum size of the states they can operate depends on the total storage supply collaboratively by all nodes instead of the storage resource of each individual node.
When more nodes joining the system, they are essentially \emph{contributing} their storage resources to alleviate the storage pressure on every node and help support even larger states, akin to common distributed databases~\cite{corbett2013spanner}.
This property would be the key to enable blockchains to operate giant big data states as distributed databases does.

Each table (or finer-grained table partition) is stored by a subset of nodes and authenticated data structures~\cite{martel2004general, tamassia2003authenticated} are employed to safely process cross table queries (\eg join operations)~\cite{zhang2015integridb,hong2024gridb}.
More general-purpose sharded blockchains follow this pattern, group nodes into multiple \emph{committees} who are responsible for maintaining disjoint state shards.
These works usually employ multiple instances of fully-replicated BFT protocols (popular choices including PBFT~\cite{pbft} and ByzCoin~\cite{kogias2016enhancing}), one within each committee, and resolve the atomicity of cross-committee transactions with specialized strategies.

Next we discuss these sharded blockchain works in more detail.
\textsc{Elastico}~\cite{luu2016secure} lays the foundation of scalable sharded blockchains.
It performs epoch-based randomization of committees.
In the beginning of each epoch, every node find a proof-of-work (PoW) solution to obtain an identity in a random committee.
Then the members of each committee run permissioned BFT protocol locally to reach consensus on the shard they are managing, and finally broadcast the consensus outcome to other committees to reach global consensus.
Because each committee randomly samples the least amount of nodes from all to ensure an honest supermajority with high probability, the committee size is fixed regardless of the total number of nodes.
As the result, \textsc{Elastico} achieves storage scalability: its redundancy equals to the committee size and is not affected by the number of nodes.

Following the success of \textsc{Elastico}, OmniLedger~\cite{kokoris2018omniledger} improves on several security aspects, including replacing the PoW-based committee assignment scheme with RandHound~\cite{syta2017scalable}, a decentralized randomness solution based on verifiable random function (VRF).
This allows OmniLedger to significantly prolong the epoch duration (to \eg one day) and reduce the storage overhead of nodes more practically than \textsc{Elastico}.
The later work~\cite{dang2019towards} further employs trusted execution environment (TEE) to assist the intra-committee consensus for smaller committee size ($2f+1$ instead of $3f+1$ to tolerate $f$ Byzantine faults), and dramatically reduces the redundancy and improve the storage scalability.
Alternative to TEE, RapidChain~\cite{zamani2018rapidchain} employs synchronous BFT protocol for stronger resiliency.
While RapidChain is less storage-scalable (its committee size is $O(\log{number\ of\ nodes})$), it explicitly claims to tolerate $\frac{1}{3}$ Byzantine faults on par with typical BFT protocols.

Beside securing committee formation through randomization, some sharded blockchains need not specify the formation to achieve their security properties, such as the blockchain database works listed above.
Monoxide~\cite{wang2019monoxide} proposes asynchronous consensus zone, which can be treated as committees that can be arbitrarily sized.
It proposes a novel PoW mining scheme that allow mining power to simultaneously work on multiple zones and tolerate up to $\frac{1}{2}$ faulty mining power globally.
SharPer~\cite{amiri2021sharper} and \textsc{ByShard}~\cite{hellings2021byshard} take a different approach and exploits the per-determined fault-tolerant clusters to provide deterministic safety guarantees, contrary to the probabilistic ones by the aforementioned works.

\paragraph{The dilemma between security and scalability.}
As shown in \autoref{tab:compare}, all previous works fail to achieve the safety, liveness and storage scalability properties at the same time.
Ordinary BFT protocols and some blockchain databases~\cite{peng2020falcondb} fully replicate their states to all nodes, ensuring safety and liveness but with no storage scalability.
\textit{BlockchainDB} and SharPer require pre-determined fault-tolerant clusters.
They can only tolerate a fraction of faulty nodes locally within each cluster, and fail when the total number of faulty nodes can overwhelm any individual cluster.
\textsc{Elastico} and OmniLedger's fixed-sized randomly-sampled committee is designed to cope with low global faulty rates (\eg $\frac{1}{4}$).
Lifting faulty fraction without adjusting committee size leads to immediate failure on safety and liveness.
To persist these properties, we have to adaptively increase the committee size according to the actual faulty fraction, ensuring the committees consist of no more than $\frac{1}{3}$ with high probability.
For example, for a $\frac{1}{4}$ faulty probability sampling \XXX nodes is sufficient, while a $\frac{3}{10}$ faulty probability at least \XXX samplings are required for the same probabilistic safety and liveness guarantees.
As the faulty probability approaching the desired $\frac{1}{3}$, the required sampling nodes come close to all nodes, and the system degrades into fully replicated one and losses storage scalability.

\paragraph{Why processing sharding does not work out?}
The failures of these previous works originate from a common crux: the number of faults that the system can tolerate is decided by the committee size, while under BFT settings we demand to tolerate $\frac{1}{3}$ faulty nodes, the number of whom is decided by the total number of the nodes.
If the committee size is irrelevant to the total number of the nodes, then we have sacrificed the security properties and can no longer tolerate faulty power that is proportional to the system scale (the rows where \emph{Safety?} and/or \emph{Liveness?} are \emph{No} in \autoref{tab:compare}).
Otherwise, if the committee size grows as the system scales up, then the storage consumption grows as well, and we lose storage scalability (the rows where \emph{Scalable storage?} isn't \emph{Yes}).
In conclusion, with processing sharding we have to pick one from the two choices and end up with undesirable trade-offs among the properties.

\paragraph{Blockchain databases.~\cite{ruan2021blockchains}.}
In blockchain databases, clients \emph{outsource} the database storage to the blockchain, which consists of the nodes with abundant storage capacity but are mutually untrusted to each other.

Scaling out blockchain state has been the recurring topic of blockchain databases.
As the blockchain databases keep operating, the size of content they store may ever grow.
However, blockchains, as state machine replication protocols, by default require each node to store a full copy of the state in order to replicate the execution individually.
Eventually, the ever-growing database will overwhelm the storage capacity of the nodes.
Some blockchain databases leverage the inherent structure of rational databases and decompose the databases into table-level sub-chains~\cite{el2019blockchaindb}.
The researches go further by utilizing authenticated data structures~\cite{martel2004general, tamassia2003authenticated} to safely process cross table queries (\eg join operations)~\cite{zhang2015integridb,hong2024gridb}.
However, more universal solutions that are compatible with single large table or even agnostic to state structure are demanded for more widely adoption.

\paragraph{Sharded blockchains}
One layer down, the blockchains can divide the stored data into shards\cite{corbett2013spanner} and each node stores only one or a subset of the shards.
However, without the whole state, the nodes can no longer process every transaction.
EIP-4844~\cite{eip4844} of Ethereum bypasses this limitation by sharding \emph{blob}, a special kind of block data only whose (succinct) commitment is fully replicated and accessible by the transactions.
This approach is not desirable by blockchain databases which requires the whole data to present in the state.

Currently, the sharded blockchain works perform \emph{processing sharding} like regular distributed databases -- each node processes a subset of the transactions that only make use of the state shard(s) it stores.
The immediate effect of a reduced processing quorum is low fault resiliency (unless when reaching out for synchronous BFT protocols~\cite{zamani2018rapidchain}), \eg, tolerating $\frac{1}{4}$ faulty nodes globally~\cite{luu2016secure,kokoris2018omniledger,eyal2016bitcoin,dang2019towards} or $\frac{1}{3}$ faulty nodes locally within each processing quorum~\cite{el2019blockchaindb,amiri2021sharper}.
