\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:bg}

\paragraph{Problem settings.}
In this work we target the BFT protocols that perform state machine replication (SMR) over states that potentially cannot fit into single node's storage.
As a BFT protocol \sys guarantees \emph{linearizable}\xxx{cite} and tolerate Byzantine faults: the processing results observed by the clients can be simulated by a sequential processing of the transactions, even if up to $f$ participating nodes' behavior is arbitrarily diverged from the protocol's specification \eg collude to cheat the other nodes, as long as the system consists of more than $3f$ nodes.
Additionally, \sys also guarantees to operate with $O(state\ size)$ storage capacity occupied across all (correct) nodes regardless of the number of nodes, while a typical fully-replicated BFT protocol would take $O(state\ size\times{}number\ of\ node)$.

We don't assume any specific structure of the state, only requires it to be compatible for \emph{sharding}~\cite{corbett2013spanner}.

The state should be able to be divided into certain number of shards
\footnote{
    In some works on sharded blockchains, \emph{shard} (or node shard) refers to the group of nodes that work on certain state shard.
    Following distributed database's convention, in this work shard means \emph{data shard} and always refers to state shard.
}, and given a transaction, nodes can identify which shard(s) are required to process the transactions.
The number of shards is also not significant, though sufficient shards (\ie much more than the number of nodes) can achieve a better load balance effect.
Examples of sharding compatible states are key-value store (atop what relational databases potentially build)~\cite{corbett2013spanner} and (Merkle) Patricia Trie~\cite{mpt} used by Ethereum~\cite{buterin2014next}.

We also do not assume any specific structure of the transactions, which may range from simple key-value updates to complex smart contracts~\cite{buterin2014next}.
Though not affecting safety, we expect to work with transactions that access only single or few shards, \ie, online transaction processing (OLTP) workload instead of online analytical processing (OLAP), for performance reason.
This expectation is valid for extensive scenarios such as token transfer between a pair of accounts or status update of a specific merchandise in a supply chain.
Besides, OLAP workload that scans over large number of shards can be performed off-chain with a verified state checkpoint (\autoref{sec:overview}).

\paragraph{The cases of big blockchain states~\cite{deepa2022survey,yang2017big}.}
It's already not news that popular permissionless blockchains are managing large states (\eg 659 GB for Bitcoin~\cite{nakamoto2008bitcoin}\footnote{\url{https://ycharts.com/indicators/bitcoin_blockchain_size}}, 1312 GB for Ethereum~\cite{buterin2014next}\footnote{\url{https://ycharts.com/indicators/ethereum_chain_full_sync_data_size}})~\xxx{find solana data}, even when the on-chain storage is insanely expensive~\cite{balduf2022dude} (3.28 ETH per MB~\cite{palau2018storing} which is over 8000USD today).

While permissioned or consortium blockchains in data center have more abundant storage resources, they demand even larger blockchain states.
Other than the well-known financial usages~\cite{qi2021bidl,lokhava2019fast}, previous works have also proposed to deploy various big data applications on blockchains like mobile crowdsensing in industrial IoT~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, edge computing~\cite{xu2018making}, crime evidences~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design}, medical and educational records~\cite{sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss}, machine learning~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain}, and intelligent transportation and smart city~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain}.
Blockchain is favored for its strict security guarantee, and has been widely researched for securing data collection~\cite{bodkhe2020blockchain,liu2018blockchain}, sharing~\cite{xu2018making}, management~\cite{kim2021two,jeong2020design,sun2020blockchain,li2019edurss} and analytics~\cite{shen2019secure,salah2019blockchain} and preserving privacy during processing~\cite{hirtan2018blockchain, sharma2018blockchain} in the environments where participates are unreliable or mutually untrusted.

These big data blockchains can incur very large states.
NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH, a dataset recorded at a trading matching engine, is reported to have 20 TB of total size and 4.13 GB per day~\cite{laughlin2014information}.
The dataset for high-frequency stock market can go as high as 20-40 GB per day~\cite{rabindrajit2024high}.
Also, the medical records are usually in MB or even tens of MB scale~\cite{garcia2011mantisgrid} due to their multimodal contents, while public medical record datasets may contain up to 40,000-200,000 records~\cite{pollard2018eicu,johnson2023mimic} and occupy TB level storage.
These are just the sizes of one-party datasets. In consortium blockchains, multiple participates (\eg exchanges or hospitals) contribute their data, each of which could be of a similar size of these datasets.
This results in TB or even PB scale blockchain states that are beyond the storage capacity of single blockchain nodes.

\paragraph{Previous arts with sharded blockchain states.}
In common centralized distributed databases like~\cite{corbett2013spanner}, all nodes are centrally managed to collaboratively store the database states and each transaction is processed only once.
While in BFT systems the processing has to be replicated in order to tolerate faults, and multiple state copies reside on different nodes to support the replicated processing, resulting in high storage overhead.
Previous works developed blockchains with sharding mechanism which allow nodes to store only partial states and reduce the storage load.
In blockchain databases\cite{ruan2021blockchains} like \textit{BlockchainDB}~\cite{el2019blockchaindb} and FalconDB~\cite{peng2020falcondb}, the state consists of multiple tables.
Each table is stored by a subset of nodes and authenticated data structures~\cite{martel2004general, tamassia2003authenticated} are employed to safely process cross table queries (\eg join operations)~\cite{zhang2015integridb,hong2024gridb}.
The sharded blockchain works are more universal and state-agnostic solutions that also provide clear safety guarantees.
In these works, the nodes are grouped into multiple \emph{committees} which are responsible for maintaining different shards of the state.
The transactions are processed only once by the committees (and replicated within each committee), similar to the distributed database.
Indeed, the works report their space efficiency especially in large scale setup.

The downside for the nodes to store less state data is that, in sharded blockchains only a subset of nodes can participate the processing of each transaction.
This has to result in some trade-offs around the resiliency to the faulty processing nodes.
An early line of works~\cite{luu2016secure,kokoris2018omniledger,eyal2016bitcoin,dang2019towards} can tolerate only up to $\frac{1}{4}$ faulty nodes.
RapidChain~\cite{zamani2018rapidchain} resorts to synchronous protocol and poses higher requirement to the network conditions.
Monoxide~\cite{wang2019monoxide} assumes the domination of professional mining facilities and is limited to PoW setup.

They either pose performance penalty and complicate the reasoning of safety (potentially relying on the client~\cite{kokoris2018omniledger} or TEE~\xxx{cite}), or the sharded blockchains give up atomicity and relax the linearizable guarantee~\cite{luu2016secure} or even completely overlook cross shard transactions~\cite{li2020polyshard}.
